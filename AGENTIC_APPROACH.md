# Our Agentic Approach

We approached the hackathon with a simple principle: **humans should own the judgment; agents should multiply the execution.**

Our team stayed responsible for the parts that required taste and context—identifying the real student problem, debating the user experience, choosing tradeoffs, and deciding what would make the demo compelling. We used agents as specialized collaborators that could rapidly turn those decisions into research, designs, technical plans, working software, and tests.

## The Process

### 1. Humans framed the problem

We began with a lived experience: meeting many people at Kellogg, then struggling to remember who they were or restart the conversation. As a team, we defined the emotional job of Peerdrop: helping a brief introduction become a lasting connection.

### 2. Agents helped us explore the solution space

Instead of immediately asking an agent to build an app, we used it as a product-thinking partner. It challenged assumptions, compared different experiences, surfaced privacy concerns, and helped us narrow the concept into a focused journey: exchange intentionally, remember through context, and reconnect later.

### 3. We created shared context before writing code

Our decisions were translated into a PRD, business case, technical specification, and hackathon-specific operating instructions. These became a shared source of truth for every agent. This reduced repeated explanation and helped keep product, design, and engineering aligned as the project evolved.

### 4. We divided work by expertise

We treated the system like a small cross-functional team:

- Product agents refined the problem, scope, and business value.
- Design agents shaped the interaction model and visual experience.
- Engineering agents implemented the frontend, backend, data model, and exchange flow.
- Review and debugging agents challenged the implementation and investigated failures.
- Testing agents verified the experience across unit, browser, database, and build checks.

Where work could happen independently, agents operated in parallel. Where decisions depended on one another, we sequenced the work deliberately.

### 5. Humans stayed in the loop

Agents proposed; the team decided. We reviewed features through the lens of the original problem, redirected the experience when it became too complicated, and continuously distinguished between what was essential for the demo and what could wait. Feedback was conversational and iterative rather than delivered as one large specification upfront.

### 6. Every iteration closed with evidence

We did not treat generated code as finished code. Each cycle followed a repeatable loop:

> **Frame → Explore → Decide → Build → Test → Review → Refine**

Agents were expected to verify their work, document what changed, and preserve enough context for another agent—or a human teammate—to continue without starting over.

## Why It Worked

This approach combined **human empathy, product instinct, and accountability** with the agents' speed, breadth, and persistence. The result was not simply “an app built by AI.” It was a human-led product process in which agents served as an elastic product, design, engineering, and quality-assurance team—helping us move from an authentic problem to a polished prototype within hackathon time.
